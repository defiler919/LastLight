# DARKWELL Narrative Foundation

Status: baseline narrative canon for ongoing story design.

This document records the currently agreed world premise, player identity, time-loop structure, settlement model, god/follower relationship, exploration structure, and first-settlement character direction. Details marked TBD are intentionally open.

## 1. Core tone and genre

DARKWELL is a top-down survival-horror exploration game with mystery, folk-horror, nonlinear exploration, and strong environmental storytelling.

The target tone is oppressive and uncanny without remaining uniformly hopeless. The game should preserve curiosity, discovery, beautiful or strange spaces, temporary safety, and human interaction so that horror has contrast.

Primary fantasy:

- Explore interconnected collected settlements and world fragments.
- Learn how each community reached its final state.
- Build relationships with a small cast during the day.
- Survive those same people, corrupted believers, and other anomalies at night.
- Push deeper into older, more degraded worlds while discovering how the worlds connect.

The game is not about defeating a god. The god must remain overwhelmingly above the player in scale and power.

## 2. The unnamed god

The central supernatural force is an original folk-horror god inspired by the atmosphere and cosmological motifs of northern Eurasian / shamanic traditions, but it must not directly copy a real-world deity, ritual, ethnic religion, or named mythological figure.

The god's true name, origin, body, motives, and background are deliberately obscured.

Different communities may call it different things. No single title is guaranteed to be correct.

The player should learn only a few reliable facts:

- It can be contacted through an old summoning/protection ritual.
- It can apparently save a settlement from an imminent catastrophe.
- Its protection is deceptive.
- Instead of restoring normal life, it removes the settlement from ordinary reality and makes it part of an extradimensional collection.
- The collected settlement then decays over time.
- Weekly sacrifice can delay that decay but cannot prevent the final collapse.
- Some followers can trade away essential parts of themselves for abnormal abilities.
- The god is not a conventional boss target and is not meaningfully threatened by the player's actions.

The story should strongly suggest that the god may engineer, accelerate, or arrange catastrophes after knowledge of it reaches a community. Disease, earthquakes, landslides, storms, war, and other disasters can all serve this purpose. The exact causal mechanism should remain partially ambiguous unless a later story decision explicitly confirms it.

## 3. How a settlement is collected

A "world" does not have to mean a planet. A village, town, district, isolated base, ship, underground settlement, or other self-contained community can function as a complete world for the people living there.

The recurring process is:

1. Someone learns that the god exists, finds an old rite, or recovers forbidden knowledge.
2. A catastrophic threat appears or intensifies.
3. Ordinary solutions fail or appear to fail.
4. The community performs a protection/summoning ritual and asks the god for salvation.
5. The immediate disaster is stopped, diverted, or rendered irrelevant.
6. The settlement discovers that it has been removed from normal reality.
7. The settlement becomes one of the god's collected small worlds.
8. The inhabitants learn that the world is decaying and that sacrifice only delays the collapse.
9. Society fragments: faith, denial, resistance, fatalism, opportunism, pleasure seeking, and ritual authority emerge.

The god therefore "saves" the settlement only in the narrowest possible sense. People avoid one immediate extinction event, but enter a closed system whose destruction is merely postponed.

## 4. Multi-world structure

Collected worlds exist at different effective depths.

Depth is both spatial and temporal:

- Upper / newer worlds have spent less time inside the collection and remain comparatively coherent.
- Deeper / older worlds have endured far more cycles.
- The deeper the player travels, the longer the local world has repeated and the more severe its corruption becomes.

Corruption can affect:

- geography and architecture;
- memory;
- population;
- identity;
- day/night boundaries;
- creatures and followers;
- the reliability of ordinary reality;
- overlap between different collected worlds.

The player should repeatedly experience the surprise that two areas thought to be far apart or unrelated are physically connected.

Deep wells are important anchors and ritual sites, but they are not the only routes between worlds.

Other possible cross-world connections include:

- old roads and tunnels;
- mining passages;
- railways;
- drainage channels and rivers;
- roots and fungal networks;
- collapsed buildings;
- maintenance passages;
- caves;
- spatial overlaps and anomalies.

Worlds must not feel like isolated portal levels. Routes, visual landmarks, ecology, artifacts, NPC history, and consequences should cross boundaries whenever possible.

## 5. Player identity: the failed sacrifice

The player is the most recent failed sacrifice.

In the player's home settlement:

- the community chose the player as a sacrifice;
- the ritual progressed far enough for the player to receive the sacrificial mark;
- the final offering was delayed;
- the catastrophe arrived before the player could be formally delivered;
- the disaster caused the player to fall into the deep well;
- because the delivery was incomplete, the player is marked but does not properly belong to the sacrificial cycle of any collected world.

This failed state allows the player to cross boundaries that ordinary inhabitants cannot.

Every collected settlement can recognize something about the mark. Believers and ritual authorities therefore tend to treat the player as a rare, valuable, unusually suitable sacrifice.

The exact metaphysical explanation of why the failed sacrifice can traverse worlds should remain compatible with gameplay and SightWeave, but the direct SightWeave-lore explanation is not yet locked.

## 6. Day/night small loop

The game uses a daily small loop.

Daytime is the primary exploration/social phase:

- explore;
- gather resources;
- trade;
- talk with NPCs;
- solve environmental problems;
- discover story fragments;
- prepare defensive positions and escape routes;
- influence relationships and the coming night.

Night is the pursuit/survival phase:

- NPCs who spoke normally to the player during the day can become hunters or ritual participants;
- supernatural anomalies arrive or become more active;
- the player must evade, defend, relocate, or exploit conflicts between threats;
- the pursuing humans and the anomalies do not have to be allies.

The working reset time is 06:00.

From Monday through Saturday, losing the night does not end the run. The player awakens the next morning and suffers a penalty such as partial item loss. Exact loss rules remain TBD.

NPCs involved in capturing, sacrificing, or killing the player can change the next day. Examples include guilt, compensation, gratitude, increased fanaticism, hostility, avoidance, or altered dialogue. This aftermath should be authored per important NPC rather than generated as generic morality simulation.

## 7. Weekly big loop

The game also uses a seven-day big loop.

Monday through Saturday are recoverable nights.

Sunday is the major sacrifice / major defense night.

Current locked direction:

- Sunday has higher pressure than ordinary nights.
- At least two major settlement NPCs attack or participate simultaneously; more may join.
- The exact group can depend on random selection, prior player actions, relationships, corruption state, and story conditions.
- Death during Sunday defense ends the game/run rather than waking the player on Monday.
- Surviving Sunday advances into a new weekly cycle.

What exactly resets at the beginning of a new week, and which world-state changes are permanent, is still a gameplay-design decision and should not be invented by narrative documentation without approval.

## 8. Settlement cast size

A normal major settlement should be designed around approximately six important NPCs rather than a large simulated population.

The small cast is intentional:

- each NPC must represent a distinct human response to entrapment and sacrifice;
- each NPC needs a recognizable daytime personality and nighttime gameplay role;
- each NPC should have an authored relationship to the local disaster, the god, and the weekly sacrifice;
- only a few supporting/background people should exist when needed for staging.

The limited cast is also part of the fiction: older collected worlds can lose, merge, forget, or distort inhabitants as corruption advances.

## 9. Human conflict

The primary human enemies are not god-created soldiers. They are people who chose to maintain the sacrificial order.

Some begin calling themselves representatives, priests, elders, protectors, or interpreters of the god even if the god never explicitly appointed them.

This is essential to the game's conflict:

- the god is effectively untouchable;
- the player fights, avoids, manipulates, or reasons with humans who have built a social order around sacrifice;
- followers may sincerely believe that sacrificing one person is the only way to keep everyone else alive another week;
- resistance exists but has repeatedly failed to affect the god itself;
- the horror should come from what people choose to do under an unwinnable cosmic pressure.

Followers should not be divided into simple "evil believers" and "good unbelievers." A caring person may support sacrifice to save a child; a cruel person may oppose the priesthood for selfish reasons.

## 10. The god's gifts: malicious fulfillment

A major character-design rule is that the god's gifts are maliciously literal.

The pattern is:

1. A person desperately wants something.
2. They ask the god for the power to achieve it.
3. The god takes away the very human thing most essential to that identity or goal.
4. The wish is then fulfilled through an abnormal replacement ability.
5. The result technically satisfies the request while destroying part of the person who made it.

The trade is not fair, symbolic, or benevolent. The god appears to choose the most cruel interpretation possible.

Possible prices include:

- limbs or senses;
- voice;
- sleep;
- memory;
- love;
- fear;
- anger;
- empathy;
- identity;
- relationships;
- the memory of a loved one.

Do not reduce every gift to visible mutilation. Emotional, relational, and cognitive losses are often more disturbing.

## 11. First settlement: currently locked NPC concepts

The first major village is planned around six important NPCs. Five concepts are currently established; the sixth remains TBD.

### 11.1 The eyeless hunter

Identity: hunter.

Wish: become able to find and hunt prey more effectively / never lose prey when the settlement depends on hunting.

Price: his eyes, the tool most obviously essential to hunting.

Result: despite having no eyes, he becomes a far more dangerous hunter.

Night role: trap and route-control specialist. He predicts paths, reads traces, and prepares killing/capture zones rather than relying on ordinary sight.

### 11.2 The armless doctor

Identity: doctor.

Wish: save more people / obtain the ability to treat those normal medicine cannot save.

Price: both arms, the body parts most essential to a physician's normal practice.

Result: she can affect bodies without touching them, but the same ability can poison, corrupt, induce sickness, or manipulate tissue.

Night role: poison, contamination, debuff, and area-denial specialist.

### 11.3 The voiceless high priest

Identity: principal ritual authority / priest.

Wish: make prayer reach the god, strengthen ritual influence, or become a more effective intermediary.

Price: his voice, removing his ability to preach, pray aloud, or recite liturgy.

Result: he replaces spoken prayer with ritual movement and dance. His body becomes the prayer.

Night role: support/ritual controller who strengthens nearby villagers and followers and can cause elite corrupted believers to emerge.

### 11.4 The legless village head

Identity: village leader.

Wish: lead, organize, or make the community follow his command through disaster.

Price: both legs, removing his ability to physically lead people from the front.

Result: he must be carried by other believers. The people carrying him can become parasitized/deformed monsters.

Night role: command-and-possession specialist. He converts or rides followers, creating different combat bodies while remaining dependent on them for movement.

### 11.5 The mother who forgot her child

Identity: mother searching for a lost child.

Wish: have the child returned / find the child no matter what.

Price: every meaningful memory that makes that child "her child."

Result: the child returns, but the mother no longer recognizes or remembers the child.

Day presentation: vacant, distracted, slightly foolish or dissociated; wanders while singing to herself.

Night/field rule: an invulnerable, very fast, high-damage child entity remains within roughly a 20-meter relationship radius of the mother and attacks adults/players entering its territory. The child is not a valid attack target. This both respects the design rule that the player does not harm children and creates a non-combat spatial threat.

## 12. Fragmented NPC storytelling

Important NPC history is not delivered as a single exposition conversation.

Current design rule:

- after the player experiences a night led or strongly defined by a specific NPC, regardless of success or failure, a new fragment of that NPC's past becomes discoverable the next day;
- the fragment usually appears in the NPC's home, workplace, ritual space, or another strongly associated location;
- successive fragments reconstruct the person's life before the god's gift, the catastrophe, the wish, the price, and the final transformation;
- fragments should often be environmental objects, altered rooms, photographs, tools, records, letters, missing objects, or recovered memories rather than only readable diaries.

The narrative effect should be that the more often the player survives a person's night, the more fully the player understands the human being inside the monster/follower.

The player may begin by perceiving an NPC as a threat and later become reluctant to kill, condemn, or abandon them.

## 13. Exploration and bosses

DARKWELL should preserve nonlinear exploration.

A major boss or powerful follower should usually block story control, a major world-state change, a ritual node, or a convenient route rather than being the only possible way to keep exploring.

Players should often be able to:

- retreat;
- go around;
- find another layer;
- reach later spaces through a dangerous route;
- discover a shortcut back to an earlier settlement;
- return later with more knowledge.

This supports the intended feeling that the world is deep, interconnected, and larger than the player's initial mental map.

## 14. Narrative themes

Primary themes currently supported by the setting:

- fear of an overwhelmingly unequal power;
- the human cost of choosing survival at any price;
- sacrifice justified as social arithmetic;
- malicious fulfillment of desperate wishes;
- identity reduced by repeated compromise;
- faith used as comfort, control, excuse, and genuine belief;
- the difference between postponing death and actually living;
- curiosity and exploration inside a doomed structure;
- human relationships retaining meaning even when the cosmic situation is unwinnable.

The story should not become a conventional "kill the god and save reality" power fantasy.

## 15. Intentionally unresolved questions

The following are not yet canon and must stay open until explicitly decided:

- the god's true name, origin, species/nature, body, and motive;
- whether the god directly creates every disaster or merely exploits some of them;
- how the first summoning ritual entered each culture;
- the exact metaphysical mechanism behind the sacrificial mark;
- the exact lore relationship between the god, collected worlds, and SightWeave;
- the sixth NPC in the first settlement;
- exact Monday-Saturday death penalties;
- exact weekly reset/persistence rules after a successful Sunday;
- whether the player can permanently rescue, free, or only escape collected settlements;
- the final ending and whether the god ever appears directly.

Any future narrative work should distinguish locked canon from proposals and should update this section when an unresolved question becomes a project decision.
